# Chapter 7: The Crossroads

One year later, everything had changed.

Jed's company, Jedson Enterprises, had grown beyond his wildest dreams. They were now handling ten containers a month, employing twenty people, and generating profits that Jed had never thought possible. He had moved out of his cramped room in Surulere and into a modest flat in Yaba — two bedrooms, a living room, a kitchen, and a balcony that overlooked the street. It wasn't much, but it was his.

He had also moved closer to Adaeze. She still worked at the customs office, but they now lived within walking distance of each other, making it easier to see each other on the rare days when they both had free time.

But with success came new challenges. The business was demanding, the competition was fierce, and the constant pressure was beginning to take its toll. Jed found himself working late into the night, sacrificing sleep and relaxation for the sake of growth.

And then, one day, everything came to a head.

---

It started with a phone call from David.

"Jed, we have a problem."

The problem was simple: a competitor had undercut their prices, stealing a major client. The client's name was Okpara Enterprises, one of their biggest accounts, responsible for twenty percent of their revenue.

"They say our prices are too high," David said, his voice tight with frustration. "They want a thirty percent discount. If we don't match it, we lose them."

Jed felt the familiar knot of anxiety tighten in his stomach. A thirty percent discount would destroy their profit margins. But losing Okpara Enterprises would be devastating too.

"What do you want to do?" Jed asked.

David was silent for a moment. "I think we should match the price. Take the hit now, rebuild later."

Jed hesitated. Matching the price meant working at a loss for months, possibly longer. It was a risky strategy, one that could bankrupt them if things didn't improve.

"I don't know," Jed said slowly. "Maybe there's another way."

"What other way? This is business, Jed. Sometimes you have to make hard choices."

The call ended awkwardly, with neither of them convinced. Jed sat in his office, staring at the wall, feeling the weight of the decision pressing down on him.

---

He called a meeting with his team — the three managers who handled operations, finance, and sales. They sat around the table, their faces worried, their voices hushed.

"We can't match the price," said Chidi (who had joined the company six months earlier as Operations Manager). "We'll be working at a loss. It's suicide."

"But if we don't, we lose Okpara," said the Sales Manager, a sharp-tongued woman named Blessing. "And with them go other clients. The domino effect."

"There's got to be another way," Jed said. "We just need to find it."

The meeting lasted for hours. They debated, argued, and eventually came up with a plan. Instead of matching the price directly, they would offer Okpara a value-added package — free delivery, extended credit terms, and a loyalty bonus for long-term contracts. It would cost them money, but less than a straight discount.

It was a gamble. But in business, everything was a gamble.

---

The meeting with Okpara was tense. The company's procurement manager, a stern man named Mr. Adeyemi, listened to Jed's presentation with a skeptical expression.

"You want us to stay with you, even though your prices are higher?" he asked.

"I want you to consider the total value," Jed replied. "Not just the price, but the service, the reliability, the relationship. We've never let you down. We've always delivered on time, every time. Can your new supplier say the same?"

Mr. Adeyemi was silent for a long moment. Then, slowly, he smiled.

"You are a tough negotiator," he said. "I like that. Very well — we will stay with you. But next year, we revisit the pricing."

Jed breathed a sigh of relief. "Thank you, sir. We won't let you down."

---

The crisis passed, but it left Jed shaken. He realized, for the first time, how fragile success could be. One wrong move, one bad decision, and everything could come crashing down.

That night, he sat on his balcony, watching the lights of Lagos flicker in the distance. Adaeze was beside him, her hand in his, her presence a comforting warmth in the cool night air.

"You look tired," she said.

"I am tired," he admitted. "But it's a good tired. A productive tired."

She leaned her head on his shoulder. "You know what I think? I think you need a break. When was the last time you took a day off?"

Jed laughed. "I can't remember."

"That's what I thought. Come with me this weekend. Let's go somewhere. Anywhere. Just the two of us."

He wanted to say no — there was always work to do, always a fire to put out. But looking at Adaeze, at her hopeful eyes, he realized that she was right. He had been so focused on building the business that he had forgotten to live.

"Okay," he said. "Let's go somewhere."
