# Chapter 5: The New Path

The British man's name was David Okonkwo. He was second-generation Nigerian — born in London, raised in Birmingham, with a slight British accent that softened when he spoke Yoruba. He had spent the past fifteen years building a career in the UK, working for a logistics company in Manchester, saving money, and dreaming of returning to Nigeria to start his own business.

"I dey look for somebody we go help me navigate this place," David said, sitting in a small conference room at a hotel in Victoria Island. His English was formal, precise, a stark contrast to the Lagos pidgin that Jed was used to. "I get money, I get market knowledge, but I no know how things dey work for here. The culture, the system, all that."

Jed sat across from him, trying to calm his racing heart. This was it — a chance, an opportunity, maybe the start of something new. But it was also a huge risk. He would be giving up the security of a steady job (even if that job was now gone) for something uncertain, something that might fail.

"What exactly you go want me do?" Jed asked.

David smiled. "I go call you my 'representative.' You go act as my eyes and ears for Nigeria operations. That means you go inspect goods, negotiate with suppliers, handle customs clearance, coordinate with shipping agents. You go be busy, but you go learn plenty."

"And the pay?"

"Initially, I go pay you 80,000 Naira per month. After six months, if e go well, I go increase to 150,000. And you go get commission on every shipment we do."

Eighty thousand Naira was less than what he had been earning at Apex. But it was still something — more than nothing. And the commission, if the business took off, could be significant.

"When do I start?" Jed asked.

David extended his hand. "Tomorrow, if you ready."

Jed hesitated for only a moment. Then he reached out and shook the hand.

"I'm ready."

---

The first few weeks were chaotic. Jed had to learn everything from scratch — how to find suppliers, how to negotiate prices, how to navigate the labyrinth of Nigerian customs. He spent his days traveling across the city, meeting traders in Alaba, Ojo, and Idumota, inspecting goods, testing samples, and building relationships.

It was nothing like the corporate world. There were no glass towers, no air-conditioned offices, no colleagues to chat with over coffee. Instead, there were dusty markets, crowded buses, and endless negotiations with traders who spoke a language of hints and half-promises.

But there was something freeing about it. Jed was his own boss, answerable only to David (who was thousands of miles away in London). He set his own schedule, made his own decisions, and most importantly, he was learning skills that no corporate job could have taught him.

"I never know say Nigeria market go be like this," David said during one of their weekly video calls. "The complexity, the — how you say — the chaos. But you dey handle am well. I'm impressed."

Jed smiled, feeling a surge of pride. "Thank you, sir. I dey try."

"Make you keep trying. The first shipment go come next month. If e go well, we go dey do more."

---

The first shipment was a trial — a small container of phone accessories worth about 2 million Naira. David had sourced the goods from a factory in Shenzhen, paid for manufacturing and shipping, and trusted Jed to handle everything on the Nigerian side.

It was a baptism by fire.

Jed spent weeks preparing — getting the necessary licenses, coordinating with the shipping agent, arranging for customs clearance. He learned that the port in Apapa was a world unto itself, a chaotic maze of paperwork, bribes, and endless waiting. He learned that nothing happened quickly in Nigeria, that everything required patience, persistence, and a little bit of luck.

When the container finally arrived, Jed was there at the port at dawn, waiting for the gates to open. He watched as the crane lifted the container off the ship, as it was loaded onto a truck, as it made its way to the warehouse he had rented in Oshodi.

The goods were inspected, counted, and verified. Everything was in order. Almost a month of work, finally complete.

"Excellent work," David said when Jed called to report. "I'm sending your payment now. And Jed? I want to start planning the second shipment. This time, we go increase the volume."

---

Three months after starting with David, Jed received his first commission — 50,000 Naira, a percentage of the profits from the first shipment. It wasn't much, but it was a sign of things to come.

He was also starting to build a network. In the markets of Lagos, relationships were everything. Jed had met dozens of traders, suppliers, and agents, and he was learning how to navigate the complex web of favors and obligations that defined Nigerian business.

His friends noticed the change in him. He seemed more confident, more assured, less like the stressed-out corporate drone they had known before.

"You change," Tayo said during one of their Saturday gatherings. "You get swag now."

Jed laughed. "Na the work change me. I learn plenty."

"Abeg, when you blow, no forget your brothers," Bola said, repeating the same joke he had made months ago. But this time, there was a different quality to it — a sense that maybe, just maybe, Jed was actually going to make it.

---

But success, in Lagos, was never straightforward. Just as Jed was beginning to find his footing, disaster struck.

It started with a phone call. David was on the line, his voice tense.

"Jed, we get problem. The second container, e get issue with customs."

"What issue?"

"Dem say we owe duties we no pay. But we pay everything o. I think dem want bribe."

Jed felt his stomach drop. Customs. The word was enough to make any importer shudder. The system was notoriously corrupt, with officials who could delay shipments for months unless they were paid off.

"How much dem want?" Jed asked.

"Five hundred thousand. Or dem go seize the goods."

It was a huge amount — almost a quarter of the value of the shipment. But if the goods were seized, David would lose everything.

"I'll handle am," Jed said, even though he had no idea how.

---

The next week was the hardest of Jed's life. He spent his days at the customs office, navigating the endless layers of bureaucracy, meeting with officials who smiled and nodded and asked for "small something."

He learned quickly that in this game, patience was everything. You couldn't rush, couldn't demand, couldn't threaten. You had to play the game, build relationships, show respect.

In the end, it cost them 300,000 Naira — less than the original demand, but still a significant amount. The container was released, the goods were delivered, and David's business survived.

But barely.

"Na war," David said, exhausted. "This business na war."

Jed nodded. He understood now. The import-export game wasn't for the faint-hearted. It was a battlefield, and only the tough survived.

But he was still standing. And that, he realized, was an achievement in itself.

---

Six months after starting with David, Jed's salary had increased to 150,000 Naira, as promised. His commission had also grown — another 100,000 Naira from the last two shipments. For the first time since arriving in Lagos, he had money in the bank.

He sent 50,000 Naira to his mother — more than he had ever sent before. He paid his rent in advance, bought new clothes, and even treated himself to a proper lunch at a restaurant in Victoria Island.

It was a small victory, but it felt huge.

He was still on the same path, still crossing the same waters, still navigating the same chaotic city. But something had changed. He was no longer just surviving — he was beginning to thrive.

And for the first time, Jed allowed himself to dream. Not just of survival, but of success. Not just of getting by, but of making it.

The future, for the first time in a long time, felt bright.
