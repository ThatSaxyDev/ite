# Chapter 6: The Girl from the Island

Love, in Lagos, was a luxury that Jed couldn't afford. Or so he had thought.

He was wrong.

---

Her name was Adaeze. She worked at the customs office in Apapa, a young woman with sharp eyes, a quick mind, and a smile that could light up the gloomiest day. Jed had first met her during the customs nightmare with David's second shipment — she was the officer who had finally approved the release of their container, after weeks of navigating the labyrinth of bureaucracy.

"You are the one who is always fighting with my colleagues," she had said, her voice teasing. "They say you are stubborn."

"I am stubborn," Jed had replied. "But I get results."

She had laughed — a warm, genuine sound that had surprised him. In his experience, customs officials didn't laugh. They demanded, they threatened, they delayed. But Adaeze was different.

Over the following months, their interactions became more frequent. Jed found reasons to visit the customs office — real business, mostly, but also the excuse to see her. They exchanged numbers, began texting, and eventually started meeting for lunch at a small restaurant near the port.

Adaeze was from Onitsha, a city in Anambra State, but she had moved to Lagos for university and stayed for the career. She was intelligent, ambitious, and unapologetically direct — a refreshing change from the subtlety and games that characterized most of Jed's interactions in the city.

"You know what I like about you?" she said one afternoon, as they sat together eating peppered soup and pounded yam. "You are honest. You don't pretend to be something you are not."

"I can't afford to pretend," Jed said. "I'm already barely surviving."

She smiled, reaching across the table to touch his hand. "But you are surviving. That is more than most people can say."

---

Their relationship developed slowly, carefully, like a plant growing in rocky soil. Jed was cautious — he had seen too many relationships in Lagos crumble under the weight of financial pressure, emotional distance, and the relentless demands of city life.

But Adaeze was patient. She understood his situation, his ambitions, his fears. She didn't demand expensive gifts or extravagant dates. Instead, she was content with simple moments — a shared meal, a walk by the lagoon, an evening spent talking about their dreams.

"You know what I want?" she said one night, as they sat on a bench at Tafawa Balewa Square, watching the lights of Lagos Island twinkle in the distance. "I want my own house. Not a big house, just something small. Somewhere I can raise a family, somewhere that is mine."

Jed nodded. It was a simple dream, the kind that millions of Nigerians shared. But in Lagos, where property prices were astronomical and the gap between rich and poor was vast, it felt like a fantasy.

"I'll get you that house," he said.

She turned to look at him, her eyes soft. "I don't need a house, Jed. I need you. The house is just a dream. You are the reality."

He leaned in and kissed her, feeling, for the first time in a long time, that maybe the future wasn't so uncertain after all.

---

Meanwhile, David's business was growing. The third shipment had been successful, the fourth even more so. They were now handling three containers a month, importing electronics, phone accessories, and other consumer goods. Jed's responsibilities had grown accordingly — he now supervised a small team of three, handling logistics, customs, and quality control.

"You are doing well," David said during one of their calls. "Very well. I am thinking of making you a partner."

Jed's heart skipped a beat. "Partner?"

"Yes. We can set up a company here in Nigeria, with your name on the documents. You will have equity, a share of the profits. What do you say?"

It was the opportunity Jed had been waiting for — a chance to own something, to build something of his own. But it also came with risks. He would be tied to the business, responsible for its success or failure, dependent on David's continued investment.

"I'll think about it," Jed said.

"Take your time," David replied. "But not too long. This is a good opportunity. Don't let it pass."

---

Jed thought about it for days. He discussed it with his friends, with Adaeze, with his mother. Everyone had an opinion, everyone had advice.

"Take the risk," Bola said. "You never know when opportunity go come again."

"Be careful," Chidi warned. "Partnerships can go sour. Make sure you get everything in writing."

"Follow your heart," Adaeze said simply. "Whatever you decide, I will support you."

In the end, Jed made his decision. He would accept David's offer — but with conditions. He would have equity, a written contract, and a clear understanding of his role and responsibilities.

David agreed, and within a month, Jedson Enterprises was officially registered. It was a small company — just Jed, David, and a handful of employees — but it was his.

For the first time since arriving in Lagos, Jed felt like he was truly building something.
