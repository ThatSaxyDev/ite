# Chapter 3: The Friends

Saturday mornings were sacred to Jed. It was the one day when the alarm didn't ring, when the ferry could wait, when the office was nothing but a distant memory. He slept in until 8 AM — a luxurious lie-in — and spent the first few hours of the day doing absolutely nothing.

But Saturdays were also when he saw his friends.

They had formed a bond early in Jed's Lagos journey, a connection forged in the fires of shared struggle. There were four of them originally, four young men from different backgrounds but united by similar circumstances: broke, ambitious, and far from home.

---

Bola was the first person Jed had met in Lagos. They had shared a bench at the ferry terminal on Jed's first day of work, both of them clutching their tickets, both of them looking lost. Bola was from Abeokuta, a economics graduate who had spent two years job-hunting before finally landing a position at a banking software company in Victoria Island. He was older than Jed by three years, with a round face, a ready smile, and an uncanny ability to find humor in even the most dire situations.

"Abeg, don't worry about the crossing," Bola had told him that first day, his voice warm with the reassurance of experience. "You go get used to am. After two months, e go feel like nothing."

That had turned out to be a lie — the crossing never felt like nothing — but Bola's kindness had stayed with Jed. In the months that followed, they had become inseparable, meeting every Saturday at a small bukateria in Surulere to share stories, complain about work, and pretend that life in Lagos was exactly what they had imagined.

---

The bukateria was called "Taste of Home," a cramped but clean establishment run by a stout woman named Mama Nkechi. It served the best amala and gizzard stew in the area, and its walls were adorned with faded photographs of Lagos in the 1980s — a city that seemed, in those pictures, simpler and more forgiving.

When Jed arrived that Saturday, Bola was already there, seated at their usual corner table, a plate of yam and egg in front of him.

"Jed! My boy!" Bola shouted, raising his hand in greeting. "You dey late o. I thought you Dey fine."

"I'm fine," Jed said, sliding into the seat across from him. "Just tired. You know how e be."

Bola nodded knowingly. He himself looked exhausted — dark circles under his eyes, a slight tremor in his hands. His job at the banking software company was demanding, requiring late nights and weekend work more often than not.

"The banking sector no be easy," Bola said, as if reading Jed's thoughts. "But the money good. In two years, I go get something better. In sha Allah."

"In sha Allah," Jed repeated.

It was the standard refrain of Lagos dreamers — "In sha Allah," God willing. A phrase that carried both hope and resignation, the acknowledgment that no matter how hard you worked, the outcome was ultimately in higher hands.

---

The third member of their group arrived shortly after: Chidi.

Chidi was the most enigmatic of them all. He was from Enugu, a software developer who worked remotely for a tech company based in the United Kingdom. He lived in a small flat in Yaba, surrounded by computers and gadgets, and rarely left his apartment except for groceries and their Saturday gatherings.

Unlike Jed and Bola, Chidi didn't have to endure the daily crossing. His work was entirely online, his meetings conducted via video call, his income in pounds sterling converted to naira at favorable rates. He was, by far, the most financially stable of the three.

But Chidi had his own struggles. He was lonely — the price of a remote job in a city where making connections was everything. He had tried, once, to attend networking events, but the Lagos tech scene was cliquey, dominated by people with connections and resources that Chidi didn't have. He had given up and retreated into his apartment, finding solace in code and the occasional online conversation with strangers.

"You know what I hate most about Lagos?" Chidi said, after ordering fufu and ogbonno soup. "E get for here. The noise. The crowd. The way everybody dey rush li ke something go happen if dem no hurry."

Bola laughed. "But something go happen if you no hurry. The bus go go. The opportunity go disappear. Everything dey move fast for here."

"Na true," Chidi admitted. "But e tire me. Sometimes I want just calm down, think, breathe. But Lagos no allow that. The city no get off switch."

Jed nodded. He understood that feeling — the constant pressure, the sense that time was always running out. In the village, time moved slowly. In Lagos, it rushed past like the danfos on the expressway.

---

The fourth member of their group was Tayo.

Tayo was different from the rest. He was from Lagos itself — born and raised in Mushin, with the accent and the attitude that came from being a true Lagosian. He had dropped out of university in his third year, deciding that the certificate wasn't worth the debt, and had instead started a small business reselling phone accessories.

His business was doing well — not spectacularly, but well enough. He had a stall at the Computer Village in Ikeja, selling phone chargers, earphones, and screen protectors to tourists and locals alike. He made enough to rent a decent flat in Oshodi and send money home to his mother.

Tayo arrived at the bukateria late, apologizing profusely, his arms laden with bags of chin-chin and groundnut.

"I brought gist," he said, dropping into the empty seat beside Jed. "Big gist o."

"What happen?" Bola asked, his eyes lighting up.

Tayo lowered his voice, as if sharing state secrets. "You know say I dey sell phone accessories for Computer Village, yeah? So, yesterday, one oyimbo come to my stall. Foreigner. British-born Nigerian. Him say him dey look for somebody we go help am setup shop for UK. Him get money, him get connections, but him no know how the market dey work for here."

Jed leaned in. "And?"

"And I tell am about myself. About my business. About how I understand both markets. And him like am. Him give me card, say him go call me next week for meeting."

The table erupted in cheers. A British connection — it was the kind of opportunity that could change everything. A partnership with a foreigner, access to international markets, a chance to escape the limitations of the local market.

"Tayo, you go blow!" Bola said, clapping his friend on the back. "When you blow, no forget your brothers."

"Abeg, no jinx am," Tayo said, but he was smiling, his eyes bright with hope. "Nothing done yet. But if e happen, e go be big. Very big."

---

They ate and talked until the afternoon, their conversation ranging from work to women to politics to the endless mysteries of Lagos traffic. It was a welcome respite from the pressures of the week, a few hours when they could simply be themselves, without the masks they wore for bosses and clients.

As the sun began to set, Jed found himself watching his friends with a mixture of gratitude and sadness. They were all struggling, each in their own way. Bola was burning out at the bank. Chidi was isolated in his apartment. Tayo was chasing a dream that might never materialize. And Jed himself was barely keeping his head above water.

But they had each other. In a city that could be cold and unforgiving, they had this — this weekly ritual, this bond of friendship, this small pocket of warmth.

"Guy, make we take pix," Tayo said, pulling out his phone. "Saturday group."

They gathered together, four young men in their best (or at least cleanest) clothes, and posed for the camera. The photo came out blurry, poorly lit, imperfect.

But it was theirs.

---

The following Monday, Jed received a message from his mother. His younger sister, Amara, had been accepted into university — Obafemi Awolowo University, to study Medicine. It was the proudest moment of their family's life, but also a source of worry. University fees were expensive, and their mother was a widow with limited means.

"Don't worry about the money," Jed had told his mother, even though he was worried about the money. "I'll send something. I'll manage."

That night, after the crossing, he sat in his room and calculated his finances. Rent: 30,000. Transport: 4,000. Food: 15,000. Phone data: 2,000. Savings: 0. There was nothing left. Nothing.

But his sister needed help. His mother had sacrificed everything to raise them. The least Jed could do was contribute.

He opened his banking app and transferred 20,000 Naira — almost a third of his remaining salary — to his mother's account. It wasn't enough, but it was something. It was all he had.

Then he lay on his mattress and stared at the ceiling, wondering how he would survive the next three weeks until his next salary.

This was Lagos. This was the dream. This was the life he had chosen.

And there was no turning back.
