# Chapter 4: The Setback

Two weeks after the Saturday gathering, everything fell apart.

It started with an email. Jed was at his desk, halfway through preparing the Shell pitch presentation, when his inbox chimed. The sender was Emeka. The subject line was simple: "Meeting - Urgent."

Jed walked into Emeka's office, his heart already sinking. His manager sat behind his desk, his expression unreadable, his fingers steepled beneath his chin.

"Sit down, Jed."

Jed sat. The chair felt cold, hard, unforgiving.

"I've been reviewing your performance over the past few months," Emeka said, his voice calm, measured. "And I've come to a difficult decision."

Jed's world narrowed to a single point. He couldn't breathe, couldn't think, couldn't do anything but wait.

"We're letting you go."

The words hung in the air, impossibly heavy. Jed stared at Emeka, certain he had misheard.

"Sir?"

"Your contract ends today. We're not renewing it. You have until the end of the week to clear your desk."

Jed opened his mouth to speak, but no words came out. He felt as though he had been punched in the stomach, the air knocked out of his lungs.

"I — is there something I did wrong? I can improve. I'll work harder. Please, sir—"

"This isn't negotiable, Jed." Emeka's tone was firm, final. "The decision has been made. You're free to use the rest of the day to pack your things."

Jed stood, his legs unsteady, his vision blurred. He walked out of the office on autopilot, barely aware of the curious looks from his colleagues. He returned to his desk, stared at his computer screen, and felt the tears beginning to form in his eyes.

He blinked them back. Not here. Not now. He couldn't break down here, in front of all these people.

He began to pack his things. Laptop, phone, notebook, pens, the small photo of his mother and sister that he kept on his desk. Everything that represented his life at Apex, reduced to a pile of objects that fit into a single backpack.

---

The journey home that evening was a blur. Jed sat on the ferry, staring at the water, not seeing anything. The ferry rocked gently, but he didn't feel it. The other passengers talked and laughed around him, but he didn't hear them.

He had been fired. After eight months of early mornings, late nights, endless pressure, and relentless sacrifice, he had been fired. Not for any misconduct, not for any mistake — simply let go, as though he were disposable.

The worst part was the timing. He had just sent money to his mother. He had no savings, no backup plan, no safety net. He had three weeks until his next salary — and now, there would be no salary at all.

What was he going to do? How was he going to tell his mother? How was he going to pay rent, buy food, send money for his sister's university fees?

The questions swirled in his head, a vortex of despair. He felt as though he were drowning, pulled beneath the surface by a current too strong to fight.

---

When he reached his room, he collapsed onto the mattress and finally let himself cry. The tears came silently, rolling down his cheeks and soaking into the thin pillow. He didn't make a sound — he couldn't. The walls were thin, and he couldn't bear for his neighbors to hear his weakness.

For hours, he lay there, staring at the ceiling, wondering what came next. The future, once so bright and full of possibility, had suddenly gone dark.

His phone buzzed. A message from Bola.

*Guy, how you dey? We go meet for bukateria tomorrow?*

Jed stared at the message. He wanted to tell Bola everything — the firing, the despair, the fear. But he couldn't bring himself to type the words. Not yet. He needed time to process, to accept, to figure out what to do next.

*Yeah, tomorrow*, he typed back. *I go dey there.*

---

The next morning, Jed woke up feeling hollow. He had slept poorly, his dreams haunted by images of failure and rejection. He lay in bed for a long time, watching the light change on the wall, wondering what to do with himself.

He couldn't go back to the village. He couldn't face his mother, couldn't bear to see the disappointment in her eyes. He had come to Lagos to make something of himself, to prove that the sacrifices had been worth it. And now, here he was — broke, unemployed, and alone.

But he also couldn't give up. Not yet. He had come too far, sacrificed too much, to simply walk away.

He got up and washed his face. He dressed, not in his work clothes, but in a simple t-shirt and jeans. Today, there was no office to go to, no boss to please, no performance to deliver.

Today, he would job-hunt.

---

Lagos was a city of opportunities — or so they said. In reality, finding a job was like finding a needle in a haystack, except the haystack was on fire and the needle was actively avoiding you.

Jed spent the morning applying to positions online — marketing roles, administrative roles, even entry-level positions that barely required a degree. He sent out dozens of applications, each one a small act of hope in the face of despair.

But he knew the truth. Even if he got an interview, even if he got a job, it would take weeks — maybe months — before he could start. And in the meantime, he had no income, no savings, no way to pay his bills.

His phone buzzed. A message from his mother.

*My son, I received the money. Thank you so much. God bless you. How are you?*

Jed stared at the message, feeling the tears beginning to form again. His mother was grateful, proud of him, believing that he was doing well. And he couldn't bear to tell her the truth.

*I'm fine, Mama*, he typed. *Work is busy, but I'm fine. I love you.*

---

That afternoon, he went to meet his friends at the bukateria. They were all there — Bola, Chidi, Tayo — and their faces lit up when they saw him.

"Jed! My boy!" Tayo shouted. "You dey look fine o. E get as e be?"

Jed sat down heavily. "I get something I want tell you guys."

The table went quiet. They all knew, from his tone, that something was wrong.

"I lose my job."

The words landed like a bombshell. Bola's jaw dropped. Chidi's eyes widened. Tayo simply stared.

"When?" Bola finally asked.

"Yesterday. Emeka call me for office, tell me say dem no go renew my contract."

"Na why?" Tayo asked. "You do anything wrong?"

"No. Emeka just say dem make decision. Nothing else."

The table fell silent. There was nothing to say. They all understood — in Lagos, things like this happened. Companies cut staff without explanation, without warning, without a second thought. It was the nature of the beast.

"Abeg, don't worry," Bola said, his voice soft with empathy. "You go find something. You smart, you work hard. Something go come."

"In sha Allah," Jed said, but the words felt hollow.

---

They sat at the bukateria for hours, talking, commiserating, offering advice. Tayo told him about a vacancy at his own company — a friend of a friend was looking for a marketing assistant. Chidi offered to help him update his resume, to design a professional CV that would stand out. Bola promised to ask around at his bank, to see if there were any openings.

It was heartening, the support. But Jed knew the truth. Job-hunting in Lagos was a numbers game, and the odds were not in his favor.

As the evening wore on, Tayo leaned in, his voice dropping.

"You know say I get something for you," he said. "But e go require sacrifice."

"What sacrifice?"

"My oyimbo connection, the British man. Him say him need somebody we go represent am for here, help am set up business. The work go involve travel, long hours, and e go pay small small at first. But if e work out, e go pay well. Very well."

Jed felt a flicker of hope. "What kind of work?"

"Import-export. Him want import phone accessories, electronics, from China, sell for UK. Him need somebody we go handle logistics, quality control, all that stuff. E go be like manager, but at the beginning e go be hard."

It wasn't a marketing job. It wasn't a corporate position in a glass tower. It was something else — something uncertain, something risky, something that would require him to start from scratch.

But it was also an opportunity. And in Lagos, opportunities were scarce.

"When do I start?" Jed asked.

Tayo smiled. "If you want, next week."
