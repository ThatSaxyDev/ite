# Chapter 2: The Office

The elevator ride to the fourteenth floor was silent. Jed stood alone inside the glass-walled box, watching the floor numbers climb. The building — Emerald Tower, one of the many glass structures that dotted Victoria Island — was home to dozens of companies: marketing firms, law firms, consultancy agencies, tech startups. The lobby alone was a statement of wealth: marble floors, crystal chandeliers, security guards in crisp uniforms.

Jed's company, Apex Marketing Solutions, occupied the entire fourteenth floor. He had been hired as a Junior Marketing Executive, a title that sounded impressive but came with a salary that barely covered his rent and transport. Still, he had been grateful for the opportunity. The job market in Lagos was brutal — for every position, there were hundreds of applicants, many of them more qualified, better connected, more desperate.

The elevator dinged, and the doors slid open. Jed stepped into the reception area, where a young woman sat behind a sleek glass desk, her fingers dancing across a keyboard.

"Good morning, Jed," she said without looking up. Her name was Adaora, and she had been working at Apex for two years. She was efficient, professional, and never let anyone forget that she was indispensable to the daily operations of the company.

"Good morning, Adaora," Jed replied, offering a smile that wasn't returned.

He walked past the reception desk, through the open-plan office area, to his desk. It was a small space — a corner desk with a computer monitor, a keyboard, and a pile of papers that seemed to grow larger every day. The desk faced the window, which offered a spectacular view of the Lagos skyline: the lagoon, the third mainland bridge, the distant silhouette of the city stretching to the horizon.

It was a beautiful view. Jed never tired of it.

He switched on his computer and waited for it to boot. While it was loading, he took out his lunch box and placed it in the small drawer beneath his desk. He would eat later, during his lunch break — if he got a lunch break. At Apex, "lunch break" was a flexible concept, subject to the demands of clients and managers.

---

His manager's name was Emeka. Emeka Okonkwo was a man in his mid-thirties, tall and well-dressed, with a smile that could charm clients and a temper that could terrify subordinates. He had worked his way up from a junior position to become Head of Client Relations, and he ran his team with the precision of a military officer.

Jed respected Emeka, even feared him a little. Emeka expected excellence, and he expected it quickly. Mistakes were not tolerated. Excuses were not accepted. Results were what mattered.

"You're late," Emeka said, appearing at Jed's desk like a ghost. He was already dressed for the day — pressed shirt, polished shoes, a watch that cost more than Jed's monthly salary.

"It's 7:45," Jed said, checking his phone. "The meeting isn't until nine."

"The meeting isn't, but I need you to prepare the quarterly report for the Oando account. It's due by noon."

Jed felt his stomach tighten. The Oando account was one of Apex's biggest clients, and the quarterly report was a detailed analysis of their marketing campaign's performance. It required gathering data from multiple sources, analyzing trends, and presenting recommendations in a clear, concise format. It was the kind of work that should have taken at least two days.

"I — I'll have it ready," Jed said.

Emeka nodded, his expression unreadable. "Good. And Jed? Make sure it's better than last time. The client complained about the visualization."

He walked away before Jed could respond. Jed stared at his computer screen, feeling the weight of the task ahead of him. He had four hours. Four hours to produce a report that would normally take days.

He got to work.

---

The next four hours passed in a blur. Jed barely moved from his desk. He gathered data from the marketing analytics platforms, cross-referenced figures, created charts and graphs, wrote analysis and recommendations. His fingers moved frantically across the keyboard, his eyes burned from staring at the screen, his back ached from hunching over the desk.

At 10:30 AM, Adaora brought him a cup of coffee — the good kind, from the espresso machine in the break room. She set it down on his desk without a word and walked away. Jed barely noticed.

At 11:00 AM, he sent the first draft to Emeka for review. Twenty minutes later, his manager's voice rang out across the office.

"Jed! My office!"

Jed walked into Emeka's office, his heart pounding. Emeka sat behind a large mahogany desk, his expression hard.

"This is unacceptable," Emeka said, gesturing at the printout in his hand. "The analysis is shallow. The recommendations are generic. And this chart — what is this? A child could do better."

Jed wanted to defend himself, to explain that he had been given insufficient time, that the data was incomplete, that he had done his best. But he knew that excuses wouldn't help. In the world of Apex Marketing Solutions, there were only results.

"I'm sorry, sir. I'll fix it."

"You'd better. I want a revised version on my desk by 12:30. And Jed? This is your job. If you can't handle the workload, maybe this position isn't for you."

The words hit Jed like a physical blow. He felt his face flush, his hands tremble. But he kept his expression neutral, his voice steady.

"Yes, sir. I'll fix it."

He returned to his desk and worked through his lunch break. He didn't eat. He didn't drink. He simply worked, rewriting sections, redesigning charts, polishing the language until it gleamed. At 12:25 PM, he sent the revised report to Emeka.

At 12:45 PM, Emeka walked past his desk and gave a single, almost imperceptible nod.

Jed exhaled. He had passed the test. For now.

---

The afternoon was slightly calmer. Jed answered emails, attended a video conference call with a potential client, and helped a colleague prepare for a presentation. The work was tedious but manageable, and by 5:00 PM, he was ready to leave.

But leaving in Lagos was never simple. There was always something — a last-minute request, an urgent email, a manager who wanted to "quickly" discuss a project. Jed waited, watching the clock, hoping that he could slip out before anyone noticed.

At 5:45 PM, he made his move. He packed his bag quietly, said goodbye to Adaora (who barely acknowledged him), and walked toward the elevator. He pressed the button and waited, counting the seconds.

The elevator doors opened, and Jed stepped inside. As they began to close, a hand appeared, and Emeka slipped in beside him.

"Early day?" Emeka asked, his tone casual.

"Just finishing up, sir," Jed replied. "I've got an early start tomorrow."

Emeka nodded, but his eyes were fixed on Jed, evaluating him. "You know, Jed, I've been watching you. You've got potential. But potential isn't enough. In this industry, you've got to be hungry. You've got to want it more than everyone else."

"I do want it, sir."

"Good. Because the next few months are going to be critical. We're pitching for the Shell account, and I need my team at their best. This could be the opportunity that propels you up the ladder — or sends you back to where you came from."

The elevator reached the ground floor. The doors slid open, and Emeka walked out without waiting for Jed's response.

Jed stood frozen for a moment, processing the words. The Shell account. One of the biggest clients in the oil and gas industry. The pitch was in two months. And Emeka was saying that Jed — the junior, the outsider, the guy who could barely afford lunch — might be part of the team.

It was either a promotion opportunity or a setup for failure. In Lagos, it was often hard to tell the difference.

---

The journey home was longer than the journey in. Lagos traffic in the evening was a beast — unpredictable, relentless, unforgiving. Jed waited thirty minutes for the bus to CMS, then another twenty for the ferry. By the time he reached the mainland, the sun had set, and the streets were alive with the evening crowd.

He walked home, his feet aching, his body exhausted. The streets of Surulere were different at night — the noise quieted, the dust settled, the vendors packed up their stalls. It was a quieter, more peaceful Lagos, the one that existed behind the chaos.

When Jed reached his room, he collapsed onto the mattress without even removing his shoes. He lay there, staring at the ceiling, letting the silence wash over him.

His phone buzzed. A message from his mother.

*How was your day, my son? Have you eaten?*

Jed stared at the screen for a long moment. How could he describe his day? The pressure, the stress, the constant feeling of being on the edge? The fear of failure, the weight of expectations, the loneliness of being far from home in a city that didn't care whether he lived or died?

He typed a reply: *Fine, Mama. Work was busy. I ate. Goodnight.*

He put the phone down and closed his eyes. Tomorrow, he would do it all again. The alarm at 4:30 AM. The ferry. The bus. The walk through the glass doors. The work, the pressure, the endless demands.

But tonight, for a few hours, he would rest. He would dream of a better life, a life where the crossing wasn't so hard, where the office wasn't so cold, where the future wasn't so uncertain.

And then, in the morning, he would wake up and do it all again.

Because that was what Jed of Lagos did. That was what all of them did — the dreamers, the strivers, the ones who had come to the city with nothing but hope and left with nothing but the memory of trying.

They endured. They persisted. They refused to give up.

And somewhere, deep in his heart, Jed believed that one day, all of it would be worth it.
