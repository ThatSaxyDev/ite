# Chapter 9: Home

The baby was born on a rainy Tuesday in August.

It was a boy — seven pounds, four ounces, with his mother's eyes and his father's stubborn chin. They named him Emeka, after Jed's late father, a man Jed barely remembered but whose legacy he carried in his heart.

The first few months were chaotic. Jedson Enterprises was growing, requiring more of his attention. Adaeze was on maternity leave, caring for the baby, exhausted but happy. They were both learning to balance work and family, to navigate the challenges of parenthood while building a business and a life together.

But they made it work. They had to.

---

Two years later, Jed stood on the balcony of his new house — a modest three-bedroom in a gated estate in Lekki, on Lagos Island. It wasn't the mansion he had once dreamed of, but it was his, earned through years of hard work and sacrifice.

Behind him, he could hear Emeka laughing as Adaeze chased him around the living room. The sound was music to his ears — the sound of family, of home, of everything he had fought for.

His phone buzzed. A message from Bola.

*Guy, we dey meet for bukateria tomorrow. No excuse.*

Jed smiled. They still met every Saturday, even though their lives had changed so much. Bola was now a senior manager at the bank, earning a salary that allowed him to live comfortably. Chidi had started his own tech company, building apps for local businesses. Tayo had expanded his phone accessory business, opening three more stalls across Lagos.

And Jed — Jed was a husband, a father, a businessman, a success.

But he never forgot where he came from. He never forgot the early mornings, the crowded ferries, the cramped room in Surulere. He never forgot the struggle.

---

That evening, as the sun set over the Lagos Lagoon, Jed stood on his balcony and watched the city come alive. The lights of Victoria Island glittered in the distance — the same glass towers he had once walked past as a broke graduate, desperate for a job.

He thought about his journey — the setbacks, the failures, the moments when he had wanted to give up. He thought about his mother, working tirelessly in the village to give him an education. He thought about his friends, who had supported him through everything. He thought about Adaeze, who had believed in him when he didn't believe in himself.

And he thought about the crossing — that daily journey from the mainland to the island that had defined his early years in Lagos. He still crossed the water sometimes, when he had meetings on the island. But it was different now. He had a car, a driver. He didn't have to squeeze into crowded ferries or wait for buses.

But he remembered. He always remembered.

---

The next morning, Jed woke up at 5:30 AM. Not to an alarm, but to the sound of his son padding into the bedroom.

"Daddy, wake up," Emeka said, pulling at his arm. "Mummy say make we go market."

Jed smiled, feeling the familiar ache of love in his chest. He got up, dressed, and followed his son to the living room, where Adaeze was waiting with a smile.

They walked out together, into the bright Lagos morning. The city was already awake, already busy, already rushing.

But Jed wasn't rushing. Not anymore. He had learned, over the years, that success wasn't about how fast you ran. It was about how far you came, how many times you got up after falling, how many dreams you held onto even when the world told you to let go.

He had come far. He had gotten up more times than he could count. And he was still holding on.

The future, he knew, would bring more challenges, more struggles, more moments of doubt. But it would also bring joy, love, and the satisfaction of knowing that he had built something meaningful.

He took Adaeze's hand, held Emeka's, and walked toward the car.

Another day in Lagos. Another day of the crossing — but a different kind now. A crossing from the life he had dreamed of to the life he was living.

And it was a good life.
