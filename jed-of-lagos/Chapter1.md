# Chapter 1: The Alarm

Jed woke up at 4:30 AM, not to the gentle chirping of birds, but to the shrill sound of his alarm — a sound he had grown to hate over the past eight months. The small phone lay on the worn wooden table beside his bed, its screen casting a pale glow across the cramped room.

He lay there for a moment, eyes still closed, listening to the distant hum of Lagos waking up. Somewhere outside, a danfo honked its horn. Somewhere closer, his neighbor's television murmured through the thin walls. The city never slept, not really — it just caught its breath between moments of chaos.

Jed sat up slowly, rubbing the sleep from his eyes. The room was small, maybe ten by twelve feet, but it was his. A thin mattress on the floor, a wooden table that served as both desk and dining table, two plastic chairs that guests never used, and a small wardrobe that couldn't hold all his clothes. The walls were painted a dull yellow that had long since faded to something between beige and regret.

He reached for his phone again and checked the time: 4:32 AM. In eighteen minutes, he needed to be out the door if he wanted to catch the first ferry from Ebute Egbatedo to CMS. Eighteen minutes to get ready, eat breakfast, and navigate the morning ritual that had become his life.

---

His mother had called it "the crossing" — that daily journey from the mainland to the island that thousands of Lagosians made every morning. She had warned him when he'd taken the job at the marketing firm in Victoria Island.

"Abeg, my son," she had said, her voice heavy with the wisdom of a woman who had spent decades navigating Lagos traffic. "That Island no be easy. The crossing, the buses, the heat — e go tire you."

But Jed had been confident then. Twenty-three years old, fresh from the university with a degree in Business Administration, and a job offer in hand. The salary wasn't much — 120,000 Naira a month — but it was a start. And the office was on the island, in one of those glass towers that glittered in the Lagos sun, a world away from the dusty streets of Surulere where he'd grown up.

"I'll manage, Mama," he had said. "I want this."

---

Now, eight months later, Jed stood in the bathroom — a narrow space with a cracked mirror and a shower head that only produced water in brief, unpredictable bursts — and splashed cold water on his face. He looked at his reflection.

The young man who stared back was barely recognizable from the fresh-faced graduate who had first walked into that glass tower. There were shadows under his eyes now, a permanent tired look that no amount of sleep could erase. His hair, which he used to keep neat, had grown wild and unkempt — he simply didn't have the time or energy to maintain it anymore. He had lost weight too; the new clothes he'd bought for his first day hung loosely on his frame.

But there was something else in his eyes too — a determination that hadn't been there before. A quiet resolve.

He brushed his teeth, using the small basin he'd bought from the market since there was no tap in the bathroom. He dressed quickly: dark trousers, a white shirt, his only tie (borrowed from his older brother), and the worn black shoes that were slowly falling apart at the soles.

In the main room, he boiled water for instant coffee — his daily fuel. He couldn't afford the fancy coffee shops that his colleagues frequented, the ones that charged 2,000 Naira for a cup of cappuccino. No, for Jed, it was three-in-one coffee, the instant kind that came in sachets, mixed with powdered milk and sugar. It was bitter, but it was cheap, and it did the job.

He drank it standing up, looking out the small window at the gathering light of dawn. Outside, the city was beginning to stir.

---

By 4:50 AM, Jed was out the door, a small backpack slung over his shoulders. Inside the backpack was his laptop — a second-hand MacBook he'd bought with his first month's salary — his lunch (rice and stew, prepared the night before), a bottle of water, and his phone.

The walk to the ferry terminal took fifteen minutes. It was a path he had memorized, every step, every crack in the pavement, every vendor who set up their stall along the way. He passed the small provisions shop where Mama Adaeze sold provisions from dawn to dusk. He passed the mechanic's workshop where cars were perpetually being repaired. He passed the church where, every Sunday, faithful Lagosians gathered in their best clothes to pray for a better life.

The ferry terminal was already crowded when Jed arrived. There was always a crowd, at any hour. Lagos was a city of crowds — millions of people moving in endless streams, like blood through veins, keeping the city's heart beating.

Jed joined the queue for the ferry. The ticket cost 100 Naira each way — 200 Naira a day, 4,000 Naira a month, just for the crossing. It was a significant portion of his salary, but there was no alternative. The Third Mainland Bridge was worse — a parking lot of cars, with journeys that could take three hours on a bad day. The ferry, despite its crowds, was faster. An hour, maybe an hour and a half, if the water was calm.

The ferry itself was nothing to write home about. It was an old vessel, its paint peeling, its seats hard and uncomfortable. But it moved, and that was enough. Jed found a seat near the window and settled in, the vibration of the engine already beginning to lull him into a light doze.

---

As the ferry pulled away from the dock, Lagos spread out before him in all its chaotic glory. The skyline was a mix of old and new — colonial-era buildings standing beside modern glass towers, slums rising up behind shopping malls, the occasional glimpse of luxury yachts bobbing in the harbor beside rickety wooden boats.

This was the island — Lagos Island, the business heart of Nigeria. Where the money flowed and the opportunities seemed limitless. Where young men like Jed came every morning, their faces bright with hope, their pockets empty of cash.

But the island was also a mirage. For every success story, there were a hundred others who had come seeking fortune and found only frustration. The rent was astronomical — a one-bedroom flat in Ikoyi could cost up to 2 million Naira a year. The cost of living was crippling. The competition was fierce.

Jed knew all this. He had seen the looks his colleagues gave him when he declined lunch invitations, when he ate his rice alone at his desk, when he made excuses about why he couldn't join them for drinks on Fridays. They knew he was broke. And Jed knew they knew.

But he also knew that this was temporary. It had to be. He was young, he was educated, and he was willing to work. Surely that counted for something.

---

The ferry docked at CMS twenty minutes later, and Jed joined the stream of commuters flowing toward the bus stops. The buses here were different from the danfos on the mainland — they were more organized, if such a word could be used to describe Lagos public transport. There were yellow molue buses, the large ones that could carry dozens of people, and there were the smaller cabs, the yellow and black Lagos cabs, which operated on fixed routes.

Jed waited for the bus to Victoria Island. It came after fifteen minutes, packed to capacity, but Jed squeezed in anyway. He held onto the overhead rail, his body pressed between strangers, the heat of their bodies adding to the already sweltering morning air.

The bus crawled through the streets of Lagos Island, stopping every few hundred slow meters to let passengers on and off. It was, it was uncomfortable, and it smelled of sweat and exhaust it.. But Jed endured He had no choice.

Another day in Lagos. Another day of the crossing. Another day of dreams deferred and hopes sustained.

He wouldn't give up. He couldn't. Not now. Not when he'd come so far.
